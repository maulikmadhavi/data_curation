# Data Curation for Computer Vision

## Introduction

This repository explains why data collection and curation matter for computer vision pipelines. It provides guidelines, best practices, and tools to ensure high-quality datasets that improve the performance and reliability of computer vision models.


## What are the sources of data?

Image and video data for computer vision usually comes from one of the following sources:

- **Public benchmark datasets** such as ImageNet, COCO, Pascal VOC, Cityscapes, and Open Images. They are well documented and easy to compare against, but may not match the target domain, camera setup, or label policy. Check the license before commercial use: many (e.g. ImageNet, Cityscapes) are restricted to non-commercial research, and image rights can differ from annotation rights (COCO's annotations are CC BY 4.0, but its images keep their original Flickr licenses).
- **Internal collections** gathered from the product or operation itself, such as cameras, sensors, logs, or user uploads. They are the most relevant to the deployment setting, but need consent, privacy review under the applicable data protection law (e.g. Singapore's PDPA), anonymisation of faces and licence plates where required, and storage governance.
- **Synthetic data** generated by simulators, game engines, or generative models. It is cheap to scale and can cover rare cases, but needs checks for domain gap before it is trusted.
- **Web-scraped or crowd-sourced data**. It is abundant, but licensing, duplicates, label noise, and overlap with public test sets must be checked carefully.
- **Data from partners or vendors**. Terms of use, annotation quality, and provenance should be agreed in the contract and written down.

For each source, record the origin, license, collection date, and any known limitations, so that later users can judge whether the data fits their task. The [Datasheets for Datasets](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.09010) template is a good starting point.


## How can we evaluate the quality of data?

Data quality is judged against the task the model must perform, not in the abstract. Useful checks include:

- **Label quality**: spot-check a random sample against a written labeling guide, and measure inter-annotator agreement (Cohen's kappa for two annotators, Fleiss' kappa for more, or IoU agreement for boxes and masks). Re-label a small gold set to estimate error rates.
- **Coverage**: compare the distribution of classes, object sizes, lighting, weather, viewpoints, and capture devices against the deployment environment. Long-tail classes are often under-represented.
- **Duplicates and near-duplicates**: use perceptual hashes or embedding similarity to find repeated frames, especially in video. Duplicates inflate apparent dataset size and leak between train and test splits.
- **Corruption and format issues**: detect unreadable files, wrong resolutions, broken EXIF orientation, and images that are almost entirely black, blurred, or overexposed.
- **Annotation consistency**: check for boxes outside the image, zero-area boxes, class-name typos, and missing annotations on visible objects.
- **Split integrity**: split by group (scene, video sequence, camera, or site), not by individual frame, so that train, validation, and test sets do not share near-identical images.
- **Model-based signals**: look at samples with high loss or low confidence, and at examples where models disagree. These often reveal label errors or hard, under-covered cases.
- **Downstream impact**: the final test of a curation step is whether a model trained on the curated data does better on a fixed, held-out test set than one trained on the raw data.

Record the results of each check in a short report that is versioned together with the dataset.


## Tools and Techniques for Data Curation

Common tools and techniques, grouped by stage:

- **Collection and storage**: versioned storage such as DVC or Git LFS, and object stores with manifest files that list checksums and metadata.
- **Deduplication and clustering**: perceptual hashing (pHash, dHash) via libraries such as imagededup, CLIP or DINOv2 embeddings with approximate nearest-neighbour search (FAISS), and clustering to pick diverse subsets.
- **Quality filtering**: blur detection (e.g. variance of the Laplacian), exposure checks from the brightness histogram, and no-reference image quality scores such as BRISQUE, NIQE, or MUSIQ.
- **Labeling and review**: annotation platforms such as CVAT, Label Studio, or Labelbox, with consensus review and gold-standard checks.
- **Label error detection**: confident learning with cleanlab, or ranking samples by model loss, to find likely mislabels for re-review.
- **Exploration and visualization**: FiftyOne, the TensorBoard Embedding Projector, or notebooks that plot class and attribute distributions.
- **Active learning**: select the most informative unlabeled samples (uncertainty or diversity sampling) to label next, which reduces annotation cost.
- **Augmentation and synthetic data**: apply augmentation only to the training split, and keep synthetic images out of the validation and test sets, so that metrics reflect real-world performance.

Pick the smallest set of tools that answers the questions above, and document each curation step so that the dataset can be rebuilt from its raw sources.
